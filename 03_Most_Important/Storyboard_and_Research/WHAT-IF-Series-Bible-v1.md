# WHAT IF? — Series Bible v1

2026-11-13

## 1. Series identity and positioning

WHAT IF? is a vertical animated series that follows one impossible change through the real world and shows, step by step, what it does to us. It is a miniature documentary with characters, cause and effect, and an ending that means something.

**Promise to the viewer:** in about 75 seconds you watch the world change, get surprised, and leave with one idea worth repeating.

**It is not:** a narrated slideshow, a sci-fi spectacle, a motivational lecture or a listicle.

**Working taglines (pick or replace):**

- One change. Every consequence.
- Change one thing. Watch everything move.

**Recognisable signatures (proposed, need your approval):**

- **The Ripple:** the instant the premise takes effect, a shift of colour and light spreads outward from one point across the frame. It appears in every episode.
- **The Day Counter:** small on-screen markers (Day 1, Week 6, Year 3) that make the timeline and the cause and effect easy to follow.
- **The Human Test:** every episode ends on one ordinary person, never on a statistic.
- **A fixed look:** the same colour grade, title treatment and two-second sound logo on every episode.

**The episode spine** (every episode, in this order):

1. Initial situation: today's world, with one human anchor.
2. The change: the premise takes effect and the world shifts.
3. The upside: what improves first.
4. The surprise: the consequence nobody predicted.
5. The cost: what breaks, and who pays.
6. The outcome: where the world settles, plus one philosophical turn.

**Premise Lock (rule):** before any script, the premise is written as one sentence stating the single change, who it applies to, when, and what stays the same. "What if everyone developed a rich mentality?" fails this test until "rich mentality" is defined as specific behaviours. "What if money disappeared tomorrow?" must say whether debts, ownership and barter also disappear.

**Content pillars** (for variety across the feed): Money and Work, Mind and Body, Technology, Society and Power, Planet and Nature.

## 2. Visual style and direction

The look is stylised cinematic 3D: expressive cartoon characters living in believable, richly lit versions of today's Earth. It is stylised rather than photoreal, and it must never imitate a specific studio's characters or designs.

**Look pillars**

- **Characters:** semi-realistic cartoon proportions, slightly enlarged eyes and hands so emotion reads on a phone, with real-feeling skin, hair and fabric.
- **Worlds:** believable materials (concrete, glass, cloth, water), lived-in detail, and clear foreground, midground and background layers.
- **Light:** motivated and cinematic, with a warm key, cool fill, soft shadows, practical lights and light haze for depth.
- **Motion:** weighty and physically plausible, with small secondary movement such as hair, cloth and breathing.
- **Format:** 9:16 vertical, composed for the phone from the first sketch, never cropped from widescreen.

**Colour script** (every episode follows the same emotional arc; each episode picks one signature hue for the Ripple)

| Beat | Palette and light | Feeling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Initial situation | Natural, balanced daylight | Familiar, grounded |
| 2. The change | Signature hue spreads in with the Ripple | Curiosity, disturbance |
| 3. The upside | Warm gold, higher saturation, soft light | Hope, relief |
| 4. The surprise | Sudden contrast: cool teal with one warm accent, harder light | Unease |
| 5. The cost | Desaturated, cold, long shadows | Weight, dread |
| 6. The outcome | Balanced again but visibly changed | Reflection |

**Avoid**

- Generic neon sci-fi cities, unless a premise genuinely requires one.
- Readable text inside generated shots. Signs, screens and captions are added in editing.
- Photoreal faces that drift into the uncanny.
- Changing the grade, line quality or character style between episodes.

**Production reality:** AI video tools are widely reported to struggle with in-frame text, hands, crowds, physics and long-range consistency. These rules are the target look. Whether a free-tier tool can hit them is untested, and it is the first thing the next stage checks.

## 3. Narration personality and storytelling tone

The narrator is a calm, curious documentarian: quietly amazed, occasionally dry, never shouting and never lecturing. Authority comes from clear logic, not volume.

**Voice rules**

- **Spoken, not written:** short sentences, one idea each, tested by reading aloud.
- **Concrete over abstract:** "the shops open, but nobody knows what to ask for" beats "the economy experiences disruption".
- **But and so, not and then:** every beat is caused by or in tension with the one before it.
- **Honest about uncertainty:** use "would likely" and "economists expect". Speculation is never stated as fact.
- **Numbers are rare:** two or three per episode at most, each checked against a source.
- **Show, don't describe:** the narrator names the idea once and the picture proves it. Never narrate what the viewer can already see.
- **Humour:** dry, human and rare, never at a group's expense.
- **Last line:** a reframe or a quiet question, never a moral lecture.

**Tone sample (illustration only, not episode copy)**

- Weak: "Without money, society would face major economic disruption."
- Strong: "Day three. The shops are open. Nobody knows what to ask for."

**Banned habits:** "Imagine a world where...", "Let's dive in", "But here's the catch", "mind-blowing", "nobody saw this coming" (unless true), and any opening that explains the video before starting it.

**Word budget** (planning estimate at roughly 2.3 to 2.7 spoken words per second): about 170 to 200 words for 75 seconds, and 140 to 160 for 60 seconds. The detail the brief asks for must come from the pictures, because the word budget is small.

**Voice casting:** warm, mid-register, measured pace with deliberate energy lifts at the hook and the surprise. Language and gender are open decisions listed at the end.

## 4. Character design principles

Every recurring character must be recognisable in under one second and still look like themselves after dozens of regenerations. Simpler, more distinctive designs stay consistent more easily than detailed ones.

**Principles**

- **Silhouette first:** each character has a different outline. Round shapes read warm, angular shapes read driven, tall and narrow shapes read anxious.
- **Three identity anchors at most:** for example a hair shape, a signature garment colour and one prop. They appear in every shot.
- **Fixed palette:** three outfit colours per character, recorded as hex codes.
- **Fixed proportions:** a set head-to-body ratio and a height chart against the rest of the cast.
- **Expressive faces:** each character has a distinct facial structure, age and heritage, and a six-expression range: neutral, joy, fear, surprise, anger, sadness.
- **Personality in the body:** one written line on posture, gait and gestures, so acting stays consistent even without dialogue.
- **Costume states:** a clean Day 1 outfit and a worn Day 90 version, so time passing is visible.
- **Authentic cultures:** clothing, homes and mannerisms reflect real places and jobs. No stereotypes, and no default to Western settings.

**Casting rules**

- Each episode has one hero, one supporting character and background crowds. More than that strains consistency.
- Background people are simplified and never need to be recognised from shot to shot.
- Characters rarely speak on camera. Narration carries the words and faces and bodies carry the emotion, which avoids lip-sync problems.

**Reference sheet standard** (nothing is generated until a character has all of these)

- Front, three-quarter, profile and back views.
- The six-expression sheet.
- Colour swatches with hex codes.
- Height chart against other cast members.
- Costume variants.
- One-line personality and the three anchors.
- A locked text description, reused word for word every time the character appears.

**Proposed: a repertory company.** Build four to six everyday people from different regions and jobs once, then reuse them across episodes in different worlds. Viewers learn the faces, and every reference sheet pays for itself many times. The cost is that the same face lives through very different premises. This needs your approval.

## 5. Environment and world-building principles

The setting is today's Earth, specific and lived-in, and it changes visibly as the premise plays out so the world itself shows the consequences.

**Principles**

- **Specific, not generic:** every scene belongs to a real-feeling place, with its own architecture, climate, vehicles, signage style and street life.
- **Rotate geography:** cities and villages across several continents, never one default city.
- **Environmental storytelling:** the background carries the consequences, such as empty shelves, new queues, shuttered shops, repaired or neglected streets.
- **Same place, two states:** revisit one location on Day 1 and again later. This is the clearest cause-and-effect device in the series.
- **Every object earns its place:** if a prop does not support the story, it is removed.
- **Scale cues:** alternate wide shots with human-scale close-ups, and use an Earth-from-orbit view only when the story needs global scale.
- **No futuristic sets** unless the premise requires them.

**Reusable location library** (built once, reused across episodes)

- Street intersection, apartment interior, kitchen table
- Office floor, classroom, hospital corridor
- Open-air market, supermarket, factory or warehouse
- Metro or train station, farm or village

**Environment reference standard** (per location)

- One master wide shot plus two alternate angles.
- Day, dusk and night lighting versions.
- State A (normal) and State B (changed) versions.
- A palette note and ten hero props.
- A one-line ambience note that links to the sound plan.

**Production reality:** crowds in motion, weather and detailed physics are the shots most likely to fail. Plan to show them through implication, such as empty space, shadows, sound or a single close detail, before attempting them directly.

## 6. Cinematography standards

The camera works like a documentary cinematographer: every shot has one job, one idea and one movement, and the first frame is already a complete image.

**Core rules**

- **Vertical framing:** 9:16, with the subject in the central 60 percent. Keep roughly the bottom 20 percent and top 10 percent free for captions and platform buttons. These margins are approximate; check each platform's current safe-zone guide before locking them.
- **Shot length:** 2.5 to 4 seconds on average, with something changing in every shot (a reveal, a reaction, an escalation).
- **One movement per shot:** push-in, pull-out, slow pan or orbit. Complex multi-axis moves are the first thing to fail in AI generation, so they are avoided.
- **Hook frame:** no fade from black. Frame one is a striking, readable image.
- **Angles:** eye level by default, low angle for power or dread, high angle for helplessness or scale.
- **Virtual lenses:** wide (about 24 to 35 mm equivalent) for places, long (about 50 to 85 mm) with shallow depth of field for faces.
- **Continuity:** keep screen direction and the 180-degree line, hold time of day within a scene, and track costume and prop state by Day.
- **Transitions:** match cuts, whip pans and the Ripple. No decorative wipes.

**Shot vocabulary**

| Shot | Its job |
| --- | --- |
| Establishing wide | Place and time, with the Day marker |
| Medium | Action and relationships |
| Close-up | Emotion and the Human Test |
| Insert or detail | The proof object: a coin, an empty shelf, a clock |
| Point of view or over-the-shoulder | Discovery and intimacy |
| High wide or aerial | Scale, and the Ripple spreading |
| Pull-back reveal | The surprise and the cost |

**Camera by beat**

| Beat | Camera behaviour |
| --- | --- |
| Initial situation | Steady, slow push-in |
| The change | Drifting, the Ripple sweeps across the frame |
| The upside | Open, rising, wide |
| The surprise | Lower, tighter, a sharp reveal |
| The cost | Slow pull-back, heavier framing |
| The outcome | Locked-off wide, then one close-up |

## 7. Sound design standards

Sound carries half the emotion, so every episode is planned in five layers, and silence is used on purpose.

| Layer | Role | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Narration | Primary information | Always intelligible and always on top |
| Ambience | Place and time | One bed per location, changing between State A and State B |
| Object sounds | Physical truth | Footsteps, doors, coins, cloth; only what serves the story |
| Music | Emotion | Three motifs: wonder (warm), tension (low pulse), melancholy (sparse piano or strings) |
| Transitions and impacts | Cinematic punctuation | The Ripple sound, risers into the surprise, hits used sparingly |

**Rules**

- **Silence beats:** 0.5 to 1.5 seconds of near-silence at the surprise and again before the final line.
- **Sound shows the change:** ambience evolves with the premise. A city where nobody works gets quieter before it looks different.
- **Music supports the voice:** it sits well under narration (a planning target is roughly 15 to 20 dB below the voice) and swells only in the gaps.
- **Signature:** a two-second sound logo plus the Ripple sound, identical every episode.
- **Works muted and unmuted:** burned-in captions carry the story with sound off, and no key information lives only in the audio.
- **Loudness:** about -14 LUFS integrated is a commonly cited target for YouTube. Confirm each platform's current guidance before locking it.
- **Phone-first checks:** every mix is tested on a phone speaker and on earbuds.
- **Rights:** only licensed or royalty-free music and effects, with the licence terms logged per episode. No copyrighted tracks.

**Open check:** if synthetic narration is used, YouTube and Instagram rules on disclosure and voice use must be verified first. That is not done yet.

## 8. Episode pacing and retention principles

The recommended target is 75 seconds, with a 60-second cut when a premise is simple. The six-beat spine does not fit comfortably in 60 seconds with the narrator explaining in detail.

**Beat map for a 75-second episode**

| Time | Beat | Job |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 to 3 s | Hook | A striking image and the premise as a reversal or question |
| 3 to 10 s | Initial situation | Premise Lock, one human anchor, the stakes |
| 10 to 22 s | The change | The Ripple, first visible effects, the Day Counter |
| 22 to 34 s | The upside | The reward; the viewer thinks "this could work" |
| 34 to 48 s | The surprise | The pivot: a consequence caused by the upside |
| 48 to 62 s | The cost | Escalation: what breaks and who pays |
| 62 to 75 s | The outcome | Settled world, the philosophical turn, the Human Test |

For a 60-second cut, fold the initial situation into the hook and trim each middle beat by about three seconds.

**Retention principles**

- **Three-second hook:** the first image must make a question impossible to ignore. Hook formulas: reversal, scale, countdown, contradiction, human stakes.
- **Honest curiosity gap:** the hook opens a question that only the final beat answers. The payoff must match the promise, with no clickbait.
- **But and therefore:** each consequence is caused by the one before it. If a beat could be moved without breaking the logic, it is a list, not a story.
- **Pattern interrupt every 5 to 8 seconds:** a new place, a change of scale, a reversal or a jump in the Day Counter.
- **Escalation:** every beat raises the stakes. No plateau in the middle.
- **One emotional close-up** between roughly 45 and 60 seconds, so the cost feels human.
- **One idea per episode:** if a script holds two big ideas, it becomes two episodes.
- **Loopable ending:** the last line or image echoes the first frame so a replay feels natural.
- **No intro, no outro:** no logo wait, no "like and subscribe", no end card.

**Measuring:** after the first ten episodes, compare retention graphs to see where viewers leave, and adjust the hook and beat lengths from that data rather than from taste. No target percentages are set here because none have been verified.

## 9. Production quality checklist

No episode moves to the next stage until its gate passes. Each gate is recorded as passed or not tested, and nothing is reported as complete without that record.

| Stage | Must pass before moving on |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Premise | Premise Lock sentence written: one change, who, when, what stays the same |
| 2. Research | Every factual claim has a source or is labelled scenario reasoning; at most three numbers, each sourced; every consequence has a stated mechanism |
| 3. Script | Hook lands in 3 seconds; all six beats present; but and therefore chain holds; within the word budget; no banned phrases; ends on the Human Test and a loop |
| 4. Shot list | Every shot has a job; no filler; no in-frame text needed; one camera move per shot; Day, costume state and State A or B noted for each shot |
| 5. References | Every character and location has its reference sheet and locked text description; palette recorded |
| 6. Generation | Each clip checked for identity drift, faces, hands, physics and artefacts; rejected takes logged; chosen takes tracked |
| 7. Edit | A pattern interrupt every 5 to 8 seconds; Ripple and Day Counter correct; captions inside the safe zone; grade matches the series |
| 8. Sound | Five layers present; silence beats placed; voice always clear; loudness checked; tested on phone speaker and earbuds; licences logged |
| 9. Final check and publish | Watched on a phone, muted and unmuted; first 3 seconds tested; facts re-read against sources; platform disclosure labels checked; lessons logged for the next episode |

## 10. Honest critique: risks to settle before episode one

The creative direction is strong, but the biggest risk is the gap between the look you want and what an Android phone with free tiers can produce. These points are my assessment, not tested facts.

1. **The look is the most demanding thing you could ask of AI video.** Consistent 3D characters, believable physics and expressive acting are widely reported weak spots of current tools. At 2.5 to 4 seconds per shot, a 75-second episode needs roughly 19 to 30 clips before any rejected takes, which is a lot for free credits. A realistic fix is a hybrid: generated clips for key moments and animated stills with camera moves for the rest. This must be decided after testing real tools, not before.
2. **The six-beat spine is cramped in 60 seconds.** That is why the target is 75 seconds. Longer episodes cost more to make, so runtime is a budget decision as well as a creative one.
3. **"Explain in detail" conflicts with fast pacing.** A 75-second script holds only about 170 to 200 words. Depth has to live in the pictures and the Day Counter, not in the narration.
4. **Popular premises are crowded.** Mind-reading, no sleep, money vanishing and AI taking jobs are familiar "what if" topics. I have not yet researched competing channels, so differentiation currently rests on treatment: the Ripple, the Day Counter, tight cause and effect, and the Human Test.
5. **Platform policy risk is unverified.** Repetitive, templated AI-made content can run into monetisation and disclosure rules on YouTube and Instagram. I have not checked the current rules. Original scripts, a distinctive voice and real editorial control are the safest defence, and the rules must be verified before you invest heavily.
6. **Speculation must be labelled.** These are scenario studies, not forecasts. Premises like "a rich mentality" can drift into motivational claims that cannot be supported, so they need precise definitions or a different angle.
7. **Audience and language are undecided.** They shape the narrator, the cultures shown, the captions and the topics, so they should be fixed before the first script.
