## 1. Executive summary

**The scenario in one line:** overnight, every adult adopts the seven rich-mentality habits as goals; nobody receives money, skills, capital or new institutions.

**The core finding.** Changing *wants* does not change *means*. The evidence says that the binding constraints on most people are capital, savings buffers, schooling, networks and the structure of local labour markets, not attitude. So the world would change a lot in **behaviour** and much less in **outcomes**, and the gap between the two is where the story lives.

**The five most important discoveries**

1. **Saving more together can hurt the people you are trying to buy from.** When many households cut spending at once, shop sales can fall before the saved money becomes investment (the paradox of thrift). Real episodes exist (Great Depression, Great Recession analyses), but how big the dip would be here is unknown. **[P]**
2. **Starting a business is easy; keeping one alive is hard, and the numbers are consistent.** In US data (BLS, verified), about 1 in 5 new employer establishments is gone after a year, and about a third are still open after ten. Only a small fraction of young firms grow fast, yet they account for the lasting job contribution of each startup cohort. A mass-entry wave would therefore produce many more failures than successes in count, and still some valuable winners. **[S]**
3. **The labour market is the self-correcting valve.** If many people leave or demand more from low-paid jobs, tight labour markets have historically pushed low wages up faster than high ones (US 2020–23, Autor, Dube and McGrew) and pushed firms toward automation (Acemoglu and Restrepo, ageing economies). But that mechanism works best in competitive private markets. Where pay is set by budgets or where work is informal (57.9% of the world's workers), the shortage shows up as strained services rather than neat raises. **[P]**
4. **Mindset alone does not lift people who lack capital.** Six randomised trials found microcredit modestly positive but not transformative. A different package (an asset, coaching and savings support) did cause lasting gains in five of six sites. For the poor, what helps is *means plus support*, not motivation. **[S]**
5. **"Everyone is a founder" already describes much of the world, and it is often not a success story.** In India, 58.4% of workers were self-employed in 2023–24 and 19.4% were unpaid helpers in family enterprises; some economists read the rise as distress, not enterprise. A rich mentality would change the *quality* of self-employment in such economies more than the *count*. **[P]**

**The three most surprising possible consequences**

1. **Shortages of employees and customers at the same time.** The new owners hunt for workers while the saving households they hoped to serve spend less. **[P]**
2. **A rich mindset might make fewer people leave jobs, not more.** If the mindset includes real financial literacy and long-term thinking, many would see that the *median* self-employed person has historically earned less than a comparable employee in some datasets, and choose to upskill, negotiate, save and run side businesses instead. The opposite pull (overconfidence about ownership) is also well documented, so the net direction is genuinely open. **[P]**
3. **Public and essential services could feel the shock first, not last.** Bus, school and clinic pay is often fixed by budgets, so it cannot rise quickly when workers drift away. The visible result is longer waits and fewer services before any raise happens. This is mechanism reasoning, not a measured result. **[X]**

**Readiness.** The research is ready to inform story development, with the limits listed in section 11 (no sector-specific evidence for transport, agriculture or sanitation; some numbers rest on secondary sources). Recommended video spine and decisions needed are in section 13.

## 2. Exact scenario rules and assumptions

### 2.1 The event (Premise Lock, extended)
At one moment, **T0**, every adult on Earth (see A1 below) adopts the following as their own goals and habits. Nothing else is changed: laws, institutions, prices, contracts, debts, existing wealth, technology, infrastructure and the natural world start exactly as they were.

### 2.2 What changes (psychology and behaviour)

| Dimension (from Bible v2) | Before | After T0 | What it does *not* give them |
|---|---|---|---|
| Financial literacy and care with money | Money handled by habit or avoided | Wants to understand and track money | Knowledge. They want to learn; they do not yet know. |
| Long-term thinking, delayed gratification | Present spending favoured | Prefers future benefit; saves first | Spare income. People on tight budgets have little to defer. |
| Investing and building assets | Often not done or not accessible | Wants to own assets | Capital, accounts, trustworthy products. |
| Spotting opportunities, starting things | Notices little; waits | Looks for gaps; willing to act | Market knowledge or experience. |
| Developing valuable skills | Trained once, or not at all | Seeks skills that pay and help others | The skills themselves. They start learning on Day 1. |
| Creating things others want | Job is the default | Thinks "what would someone pay for?" | Customers. Demand is whatever it was. |
| Financial independence and lasting wealth | Distant or abstract | A concrete life goal | Time. Independence takes years at the earliest. |

**What it is not (carried over from Bible v2):** greed, status obsession, materialism, or wanting money without creating value.

### 2.3 What does not change (hard constraints)
Capital and credit supply; who owns land, homes and firms; existing debts and rents; prices and wages on Day 1; physical infrastructure; the number of hours in a day; people's health, caring duties, culture and family obligations; legal and tax rules; the state of technology; public budgets; and the starting inequality documented in section 3D.

### 2.4 People interpret the shift differently
The premise does not make everyone act the same. Six working archetypes (for modelling and for casting background crowds). **No population shares are given; none could be sourced.**

| Archetype | How they read "rich mentality" | Typical move |
|---|---|---|
| **Builders** | Make something people pay for | Stall, shop, service, freelance work |
| **Climbers** | Get paid more where they are | Negotiate, upskill, change employer, take extra shifts |
| **Savers-investors** | Grow money steadily | Cut spending, open accounts, buy funds or assets |
| **Switchers** | Move into scarcer, better-paid skills | Retrain for trades or technical work |
| **Stewards** | Security first | Stay put, cut costs, build a buffer |
| **Climbers-of-status (drift)** | Win visibly against others | Compare, over-work, chase signals of success |

### 3E. Technology and productivity
No direct evidence exists on what a global entrepreneurship surge would do to productivity or AI adoption. The usable evidence is indirect: reallocation toward fast-growing young firms drives productivity (Decker et al.); labour scarcity speeds automation (Acemoglu and Restrepo); AI adoption by firms was in the single digits in OECD data in 2023, with exposure to automation large but displacement not yet visible (OECD). **Treat any claim about net productivity in this scenario as [X].**

**Key regional insight (the "default is already founder" point).** Where self-employment is already the default (informal economies, India), a rich mentality improves the *content* of work (pricing, saving, skills, products). Where wage jobs dominate, the shift mainly changes *whether people stay in jobs* and what employers must offer. The video should therefore **not** assume one pattern is "the world". Choose a setting and say so on screen.

**India-specific caution.** India is not one story: urban and rural, men and women, formal and informal differ sharply (PLFS). Use India as one lens, not as the template.

| L13 | Advantage converts mindset to outcomes faster for the resourced | Capital, education, networks matter | **[S]** | Microcredit vs Graduation; mobility; WIR. |
| L14 | Skills upgrade; reallocation raises productivity in some places | Workers learn; capital moves to better firms | **[P]** | Decker et al.; *no* evidence of the net effect. |
| L15 | Overwork and health costs for a subset | Long hours | **[P]** | WHO/ILO. |
| L16 | Comparison pressure and strained relationships | Visible success; time scarcity | **[X]** | Weak, mixed evidence; money-priming not replicated. |
| L17 | Institutions react (consumer protection, safety nets, pay rules) | Political response | **[X]** | Not modelled (A4). |

### 5.2 Feedback loops (why it does not run away)

| Loop | Type | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| **Wage valve** | Balancing | Quits → vacancies → wage rises → fewer quits. Strongest in competitive private markets. |
| **Shake-out** | Balancing | Too many copycat stalls → profits fall → weakest close → survivors stabilise. |
| **Demand dip** | Reinforcing, then balancing | Saving cuts spending → sales fall → incomes fall → *more* saving, until investment or pricing re-balances. |
| **Automation response** | Reinforcing | Persistent labour scarcity → automation → fewer entry-level jobs in that task → pressure on those workers. Slow and uneven. |
| **Compounding advantage** | Reinforcing | Those with capital and networks reinvest faster → gap widens. |
| **Comparison spiral** | Reinforcing (small subset) | Visible success → imitation → overwork. Weak evidence. |

### 5.3 The central uncertainty
Do **more** people leave employment under a rich mentality, or **fewer**?
- **More:** goal of ownership, plus overconfidence, plus visible success, pull people toward starting.
- **Fewer:** financial literacy and long-term thinking make the *median* risks visible (mixed founder-earnings evidence, high exit rates), and people choose raises, skills and side businesses instead.
The honest answer is: *both, in different people*, and the net direction is **uncertain**. A good story can use that tension.

**Developments common to all three [S/P]**
1. The mindset outruns the means: behaviour changes first, outcomes later and unevenly.
2. High exit rates among new firms; a minority of winners.
3. Competition for workers, with raises where employers can pay and automation where they cannot.
4. Persistent structural inequality; no scenario ends poverty.
5. A cost in time and health for a subset.
6. A higher-skill, more commercial culture by year 10.

**Which elements are best supported:** survival base rates, the labour-market valve, the failure of credit-only or mindset-only help for the poor, and persistent inequality. **Which need assumptions:** net productivity, the speed of automation, comparison effects, and any institutional response.

## 8. Answers to the ten major economic questions

**1. If everyone wants to become wealthy, who performs essential jobs?** *[P]*
Mostly the same people. Most essential workers cannot quit because they have no buffer (only about 56% can raise emergency money in LMICs). Others become "climbers": they stay, upskill and negotiate. Some switch into better-paid scarce trades. Where pay is set by budgets (public transport, schools, clinics), the exits show up as **longer waits and fewer services** before any raise. The baseline is already stressed: WHO projects an 11-million health-worker shortfall by 2030; UNESCO projects a 44-million teacher gap with attrition rising. *Mechanism: outside options rise; employers and governments adjust slowly.*

**2. Would wages rise in industries struggling to attract workers?** *[P]*
Where employers compete and can pass on costs, yes: US low-wage pay rose fastest after 2020, partly via competition (Autor et al.). Where wages are set by budget or the work is informal (57.9% of workers globally; 88.6% in LDCs), the response is weaker and slower. A rich-minded workforce would also bargain harder, which could amplify the effect. The evidence is correlational and US-based.

**3. Would businesses automate more aggressively?** *[P]*
Where labour is scarce *and* technology is feasible and affordable, yes (Acemoglu and Restrepo; WEF: 40% of employers expect reductions where tasks can be automated). For hands-on essential work, no study I found shows full replacement within a decade. The OECD finds little employment loss from AI so far. For the video: **a small, uncertain trial only** (Bible v2 rule).

**4. Would the number of businesses grow faster than customer demand?** *[P]*
In crowded, low-capital categories (food, resale, simple services), very likely for a while: entry is cheap, demand starts flat, and saving may soften demand further. Total demand can recover if investment and productivity rise, but nothing guarantees it. *Mechanism: many sellers, the same buyers.*

**5. Could mass entrepreneurship create more failures than successes?** *[S for the base rates, P for this scenario]*
By count, yes. In US data about 20% of new employer establishments close within a year, about half within five and about two-thirds within ten. A small minority grows fast and creates most lasting jobs. Failure is not always catastrophic (people learn and re-enter), but it costs more for those without a cushion. Founder earnings evidence is mixed.

**6. Would investing become more widespread, and what could happen to asset prices?** *[S for participation, X for prices]*
Participation would rise where access exists (India shows how fast it can happen). Asset-price effects are **unknown**: more buyers can raise prices if supply is fixed, but savings that fund new investment can expand supply; the "savings glut" literature is contested. Newcomers with high ambition and little experience are exposed to scams and losses (the India derivatives figure is V3). Do not state a price direction.

**7. Would inequality disappear, remain, or worsen?** *[S that it remains; P on direction]*
It **remains**. Mindset does not create collateral, schooling or networks. Those with resources convert it faster (Graduation vs microcredit; OECD mobility; WIR: top 10% own about 75% of wealth). Income inequality at the *bottom* could compress in tight labour markets, while wealth inequality likely does not fall within a decade. Nothing supports "inequality ends".

**8. Could people damage health and relationships through financial focus?** *[P for health, X for relationships]*
For a subset, yes: long hours are linked to stroke and heart disease (WHO/ILO), and burnout is a recognised workplace syndrome. On relationships the evidence is weak: materialism is linked to lower well-being (correlational), social comparison has small and varied effects, and the famous "money makes people less social" finding **did not replicate**. Show it as one person's story (Sam), not as a claim about society.

**9. What happens to people who want financial independence but lack capital or opportunity?** *[S]*
Mindset alone gives modest results (microcredit: modestly positive, not transformative). What worked for the very poor was a **bundle** of an asset, support and savings (benefits exceeded costs in five of six trials). Without that, they rely on informal self-employment, family help and local savings arrangements. The financing gap for small firms in emerging economies was estimated at $5.7 trillion.

**10. Would society become more productive, or would everyone compete for the same limited opportunities?** *[P]*
Both. Productivity gains come from skills, investment and reallocation toward the few fast-growing firms. Competition for the same demand is the default for copycats. Which dominates depends on whether people *create new value* (Bible v2's first test). Dynamism and high-growth young firms have been declining in the US since 2000, so more entry does not by itself produce more growth. No evidence supports a net GDP number.

## 10. Misconceptions and claims to avoid

| Misleading assumption | Why it misleads | Evidence | Safer framing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everyone becoming ambitious means everyone becomes successful. | Outcomes depend on capital, skills, networks and luck. | Microcredit vs Graduation; mobility; WIR | "Wanting more is not the same as being able to." |
| More businesses automatically mean more wealth. | Entry is not growth; most exit; few scale. | BLS/SBA; Decker et al. | "More starts, fewer survivors." |
| Everyone investing automatically makes everyone richer. | Returns are uncertain; prices are not guaranteed; newcomers lose. | Savings-glut debate; India participation without proven gains | Do not claim price effects. |
| Poverty disappears because people change their mindset. | Constraints are structural. | Findex ("lack of money"); WIR; Graduation | Never state poverty ends (Bible rule). |
| Everyone stops working normal jobs. | Most cannot; many choose to climb instead. | Findex resilience; labour-market evidence | Show people staying, bargaining, switching. |
| Automation solves labour shortages. | Slow, partial, task-specific. | OECD (little effect so far); Acemoglu | Show only a small trial. |
| Financial literacy alone eliminates inequality. | Effects are modest, decay and are weaker for the poor in some studies. | Fernandes et al.; Kaiser et al. | "Knowing helps; it doesn't replace capital." |
| Wealth creation has no social or environmental cost. | Time, health and local costs are real. | WHO/ILO. *Environmental costs not researched here.* | Show human costs; avoid claims on the environment. |
| Patience or saving alone predicts success. | Replication shrinks the link; contested. | Watts et al. | Avoid the marshmallow story as proof. |
| Thinking about money makes people cold. | Original lab finding failed to replicate. | Rohrer et al.; Many Labs | Show Sam's pressure as a personal choice. |
| Entrepreneurs earn less than employees (or more). | Evidence is mixed and type-dependent. | Hamilton; Levine and Rubinstein; Sorgner and Fritsch | Don't state either. |
| "90% of startups fail in the first year." | Common claim; official data show about 20% in year one for US employer establishments. | BLS | Use the BLS numbers with "US" and "establishments". |
| "Small businesses create most jobs." | The driver is **young** firms, and mostly a few high-growth ones. | Decker et al. | Say "a few fast-growing young firms." |
| "Money buys happiness up to $75,000 only." | The 2023 adversarial study says it keeps rising for most; flattens for the unhappiest. | Killingsworth et al. | Avoid any plateau number. |
| "Self-employment growth in India means an entrepreneurship boom." | Part is unpaid family work; interpretations differ. | PLFS; Ghatak et al. | Use with a neutral caption. |
| "2023 is still the record for business applications." | Superseded by 2025. | Census BFS via FRED | Avoid "record". |
| "Everyone's rich mentality means everyone is greedy." | The project defines it as value-creating. | Bible v2 | Keep the three tests. |

## 11. Research limitations and uncertainties

1. **No direct evidence.** No real case of a simultaneous, global mindset change exists (A10). Every link is an inference from analogous evidence.
2. **No quantitative model.** I built a structured qualitative simulation. I did not model GDP, employment or inequality and make no such forecasts.
3. **Evidence is uneven.** Firm-survival, job-creation and wage-compression evidence is mostly **US**; financial-inclusion data are global; essential-sector and informal-economy evidence is thin.
4. **Sectors not researched in depth:** agriculture, sanitation, construction, transport, manufacturing. Emeka's thread is reasoned from mechanisms.
5. **How I read sources.** I fetched two pages in full (BLS and ILO). Most other sources I read as abstracts, press releases or search excerpts. Check original text before relying on any single number.
6. **Secondary-source figures (V3)** include: SEBI household demat share (8.5%), the 91% derivatives-loss figure, Census business-application totals other than the seasonally adjusted series, and the 2024 marshmallow follow-up and 2020 critique. **Not for on-screen use until verified.**
7. **Conflicts between sources** are noted where they arose (Findex emergency-fund figures; Autor et al.'s "share undone"; OECD mobility averages; one source's own inconsistent 2019-to-2025 growth figure).
8. **Dates.** Several figures are old (OECD mobility, 2018; MSME gap, 2019 data). A newer MSME update was expected but not found.
9. **India literacy figure.** The 27% figure comes from the 2018–19 NCFE survey via secondary reports (and one report oddly splits it as 27% men and 21% women, which conflicts with 27% overall). I did not extract it from the survey's own tables. It also should not be compared directly with the OECD's 34%, since the methods differ.
10. **Leads not yet verified:** a Belgian administrative-data study finding former entrepreneurs earn about 27% less than matched employees for years after returning to paid work (source details not captured); GEM global necessity/opportunity shares; the second-generation MSME gap estimate; environmental costs of wealth creation.
11. **Not modelled:** policy responses (A4), migration, climate, geopolitics, and children and teenagers (A1).

### 13.4 Honest critique of the two candidate messages
- **Message 1** ("Ambition is a tool. It builds when it serves people and hollows out when it replaces them."): emotionally strong and fits Asha and Sam. But the "hollows out" half rests on **speculative** social evidence (3F); carry it through one person, not society. **Risk of misreading:** "replaces them" can sound like automation replacing workers, which is a different thread (Emeka). Reword or clarify.
- **Message 2** ("Wanting more only becomes wealth for a society when it becomes something other people need."): **better supported** by the economics (value creation, copycat stalls, necessity entrepreneurship, demand constraints) and easier to defend, but less personal.
- **Recommendation:** keep Message 1 as the emotional frame (as you prefer) and let Message 2's economic logic drive the middle of the story, so the ending is both felt and defensible. The final wording should still emerge from the script.
